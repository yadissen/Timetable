⚡ Critical Concept: Energy vs Exergy

Energy cannot be created or destroyed (1st Law of Thermodynamics). Exergy is the maximum useful work that can be extracted from energy as it comes into equilibrium with surroundings (2nd Law). Exergy can be destroyed but cannot be created!

Key Distinction:

  • Electricity = 100% Exergy (can do work directly)
  • Heat ≠ 100% Exergy (depends on temperature)
    • At very high T: 1J heat ≈ 1J exergy
    • Near ambient T: 1J heat ≈ 0J exergy
🔑 Exergy from Heat (Carnot Efficiency)

B = Q × (1 - T₀/T_heat)

Where:

  • B = Exergy (J)
  • Q = Heat energy (J)
  • T₀ = Ambient temperature (typically 280-300 K)
  • T_heat = Temperature at which heat supplied (K)

Carnot Efficiency: η = 1 - T₀/T_heat

1.1 PUMPED HYDRO ENERGY STORAGE (PHES)

Pumped Hydro

⚠️ EXTREMELY HIGH EXAM FREQUENCY

Tested in: 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021A, 2021B, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025

Typical marks: 5-20 marks | Question types: Calculate mass/volume of water, power required, energy stored

Pumped Hydro Energy Storage

Most common grid-scale storage worldwide. Water is pumped to upper reservoir using cheap electricity, then released through turbines to generate power on demand. Delivers very competitive $/kWh for large-scale applications (100s-1000s MWh).

⭐ PRIMARY FORMULA: Gravitational Potential Energy
E = m · g · h

Where:

  • E = Energy (J)
  • m = Mass of water (kg)
  • g = 9.81 m/s²
  • h = Height difference (m)

Related: m = ρV where ρ = 1000 kg/m³ for water

Power Output/Input
P = ρ · g · Q · h · η
P = E / t (for constant power over time t)

Where Q = volumetric flow rate (m³/s), η = efficiency (0.70-0.85)

💡 Essential Conversions for PHES Calculations:

  • 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J = 3.6 MJ
  • 1 MWh = 3.6 × 10⁹ J = 3.6 GJ
  • Volume of water: V = m/ρ where ρ = 1000 kg/m³
  • 1 m³ water = 1000 kg = 1 tonne
📝 Standard Exam Question Pattern

Given: Energy to store (kWh), height (m), time to charge/discharge

Find: Mass of water, volume of reservoir, power of pump

Solution Steps:

  1. Convert energy to Joules: E(J) = kWh × 3.6×10⁶
  2. Calculate mass: m = E/(g×h)
  3. Calculate volume: V = m/1000 (gives m³)
  4. Calculate power: P = E/time (convert time to seconds)

Worked Example (EP2021B, Q1a - 20 marks)

Question: A water-tower beside a lake must store 1 kWh exergy. Water surface when half-full is 40m above lake. Tank can charge/discharge in 30 min. Find water volume and pump power.

Solution:

  • Step 1: E = 1 kWh = 3.6 × 10⁶ J
  • Step 2: m = E/(gh) = 3.6×10⁶/(9.81×40) = 9,174 kg ≈ 9.17 tonnes
  • Step 3: V = m/ρ = 9174/1000 = 9.174 m³
  • Step 4: P = E/t = 1 kWh / 0.5 h = 2 kW

Answer: Tank holds 9.17 m³ water; pump power = 2 kW

Key Characteristics

ParameterValue
Round-trip efficiency70-85%
Response time< 1 minute
Lifetime50-100+ years
Energy scale100s-1000s MWh
Capital costCompetitive for GWh scale

System Configurations

  • Two-reservoir (most common): Separate upper/lower reservoirs, channels drilled through rock
  • Seawater PHES: Ocean as lower reservoir (e.g., Okinawa Yanbaru: 214 MWh, 31.4 MW)
  • Variable geometry (KOPS II): Separate pump/turbine, can run hydraulic short-circuit for grid balancing

1.2 FLYWHEEL ENERGY STORAGE

Flywheels

⚠️ CRITICAL EXAM TOPIC - APPEARS IN ~90% OF PAPERS

Tested in: 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2021A, 2021B, 2022, 2023, 2024, 2025

Marks: 10-30 per question | Types: Calculate v_max, mass, energy, compare to other storage

Flywheel Energy Storage

Stores energy as rotational kinetic energy in spinning rotor. Used for centuries to smooth torque pulsations. Modern flywheels use composite materials at high speeds (10,000+ rpm) in vacuum with magnetic bearings.

⭐ KINETIC ENERGY (Two Forms - Know Both!)
E = ½ I ω2 = ½ m v2

Where:

  • I = Moment of inertia (kg·m²)
  • ω = Angular velocity (rad/s)
  • m = Mass (kg)
  • v = Peripheral (tangential) velocity (m/s)

Relationship: v = ω × r (where r = radius)

⭐⭐⭐ MOST IMPORTANT FLYWHEEL FORMULA
vmax = σmaxρ

Maximum peripheral speed limited by hoop stress.

  • σmax = Maximum allowable stress (Pa or MPa)
  • ρ = Material density (kg/m³)

Critical Insight: Best materials have high σmax/ρ ratio (strength-to-density). This is why composites beat steel!

⭐ MAXIMUM ENERGY STORED (Independent of Density!)
Emax = σmax × V2

Where V = volume of structural material (m³)

Why this matters: For same stress limit and volume, energy storage is INDEPENDENT of density. But lighter materials allow higher v, so better specific energy (J/kg).

⚠️ COMMON EXAM MISTAKE: Students forget to convert MPa to Pa!

1 MPa = 10⁶ Pa = 1,000,000 Pa

Example: σ_max = 1000 MPa = 1000 × 10⁶ Pa = 1 × 10⁹ Pa (1 GPa)

📝 STANDARD FLYWHEEL CALCULATION METHOD

Given: Energy to store, material properties (σ_max, ρ)

Find: Mass of flywheel, maximum speed

Universal 3-Step Solution:

  1. Calculate v_max: v_max = √(σ_max/ρ) [CHECK UNITS!]
  2. Use E = ½mv²: Rearrange to m = 2E/v²
  3. Convert as needed: ω = v/r if angular velocity asked

Worked Example (EP2021B, Q1b - 20 marks)

Question: Flywheel stores 1 kWh. Material: σ_max = 1000 MPa, ρ = 2500 kg/m³. Find rotor mass.

Solution:

  • Step 1: Convert σ_max = 1000 MPa = 1000×10⁶ Pa = 10⁹ Pa
  • Step 2: v_max = √(σ_max/ρ) = √(10⁹/2500) = √(4×10⁵) = 632.46 m/s
  • Step 3: E = 1 kWh = 3.6×10⁶ J
  • Step 4: m = 2E/v² = 2×3.6×10⁶/(632.46)² = 18.0 kg

Answer: m_rotor = 18.0 kg

Additional Formulas

Power & Torque

P = τ × ω (Power = Torque × Angular velocity)

τ = I × α (Torque = Inertia × Angular acceleration)

Strain Energy (Usually Negligible)

E_strain = (σ² × V) / (2E')

Where E' = Young's modulus. Typically << kinetic energy.

Solid Annulus vs Thin Rim

  • Thin rim (Ri ≈ Ro): All material at same radius, pure hoop stress, most efficient
  • Solid annulus: Has inner/outer radius, both radial & hoop stresses. Material utilization ~1.5× better than pure hoop when Ri/Ro small

🔵 Less Commonly Tested: Solid disk stress distributions, external rotor machines with magnets, detailed moment of inertia calculations for complex geometries

1.3 COMPRESSED AIR ENERGY STORAGE (CAES)

Compressed Air Energy Storage

⚠️ FREQUENTLY TESTED - COMPLEX CALCULATIONS

Tested in: 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2019, 2020, 2022, 2023

Marks: 15-50 per question | Types: D-CAES vs A-CAES comparison, isothermal vs adiabatic work, exergy calculations

CAES: An EXERGY Storage System

CAES compresses air into underground caverns/tanks. When power needed, air is heated and expanded through turbines. Critical concept: CAES stores exergy in TWO ways: (1) compressed air pressure, (2) thermal energy. Sophisticated designs store most exergy as high-grade heat (cheaper than storing pressure).

Two Main Types

Diabatic CAES (D-CAES)

Existing plants: Huntorf (Germany, 1978), McIntosh (USA, 1991)

  • Isothermal compression (heat rejected to ambient - wasted)
  • Natural gas combustion for reheating during expansion
  • Round-trip efficiency: 40-50%
  • Uses fuel, but 60% less than gas turbine alone

Adiabatic CAES (A-CAES)

No existing commercial plants (still developmental)

  • Zero net heat input - all compression heat stored
  • Thermal store returns heat during expansion
  • Round-trip efficiency: 60-70% (theoretical)
  • No fuel consumption
⭐ ADIABATIC PROCESS (Q = 0, No Heat Transfer)

Temperature-Pressure Relationship:

T₂/T₁ = (p₂/p₁)^((γ-1)/γ)

Work Done:

W = (γ/(γ-1)) × p₁V₁ × [(p₂/p₁)^((γ-1)/γ) - 1]

Where γ = 1.4 for air, (γ-1)/γ = 0.286

⭐ ISOTHERMAL PROCESS (T = constant)

Pressure-Volume: p₁V₁ = p₂V₂

Work Done:

W = p₁V₁ × ln(p₂/p₁) = nRT × ln(p₂/p₁)

Note: ln = natural logarithm

💡 KEY INSIGHT: Isothermal vs Adiabatic

For same pressure ratio, isothermal expansion ALWAYS recovers more work than adiabatic. Why? Adiabatic process cools the gas as it expands, reducing available energy.

Work Ratio: W_adiabatic/W_isothermal = r^0.286 / ln(r)

  • At r=10: ratio = 1.31 (adiabatic gives 31% more work, needs preheating)
  • At r=100: ratio = 1.84
⭐ PRESSURE EXERGY (Critical for CAES)

B = p₀V₀ × ln(p_max/p₀)

Where:

  • B = Pressure exergy (J)
  • p₀ = Ambient pressure (typically 101,325 Pa)
  • V₀ = Volume at ambient pressure (m³)
  • p_max = Maximum storage pressure (Pa)

Storage Types: Isobaric vs Isochoric

TypePressureImplementationBest For
Isobaric Constant Underwater bags, deep salt caverns (geostatic pressure) Grid-scale storage
Isochoric Variable Rigid pressure vessels Small-scale only
📝 EXAM QUESTION TYPE: Compare & Calculate

Common questions:

  1. Distinguish isobaric/isochoric (2014, 8 marks): Define, advantages/disadvantages, implementation
  2. Multi-stage compression advice (2014, 7 marks): Single vs multi-stage with intercooling, temperature limits
  3. Calculate work/exergy (2019, 2020, 2022): Isothermal work, adiabatic temperature rise, exergy split

Worked Example (EP2019 - Multi-stage Compression)

Question: 5-stage compression to 120 bar from 1 bar. Equal pressure ratios, cooling to ambient after each stage. Calculate temperature rise and work per stage.

Solution:

  • Step 1: Total ratio r = 120/1 = 120
  • Step 2: Per-stage ratio r_stage = (120)^(1/5) = 2.605
  • Step 3: Temperature rise: T₂/T₁ = (2.605)^0.286 = 1.298
  • If T₁ = 290K, then T₂ = 376.5K (rise of 86.5K)
  • Step 4: Work per stage per kg: W = c_p × (T₂-T₁) = 1005 × 86.5 = 87 kJ/kg

🔵 Not Previously Tested: Detailed thermocline storage calculations, specific CAES plant configurations, underwater dual-pressure MPAS/HPAS systems

1.4 THERMAL ENERGY STORAGE (TES)

Thermal Energy Storage

⚠️ MODERATELY TESTED - EXERGY CALCULATIONS CRITICAL

Tested in: 2014, 2015, 2016, 2019

Marks: 20-50 per question | Focus: Exergy calculations with changing temperature, sensible vs latent heat

Three Types of Thermal Storage
  1. Sensible Heat: Energy stored by changing temperature (no phase change)
  2. Latent Heat: Energy stored through phase change (solid↔liquid) at constant temperature
  3. Thermochemical: Energy stored in reversible chemical reactions (emerging technology)

Sensible Heat Storage

⭐ SENSIBLE HEAT ENERGY

Q = m × c_p × ΔT

Where:

  • Q = Heat energy (J)
  • m = Mass (kg)
  • c_p = Specific heat capacity (J/kg·K)
  • ΔT = Temperature change (K)
⭐⭐⭐ EXERGY FROM SENSIBLE HEAT (Variable Temperature)

B = m × c_p × [ΔT - T₀ × ln(T_hot/T_cold)]

Or in integral form for variable c_p:

B = m ∫[T_cold to T_hot] c_p(T) × (1 - T₀/T) dT

This formula appears in MANY exam questions! Master it!

Latent Heat Storage (Phase Change Materials - PCM)

⭐ LATENT HEAT ENERGY

Q = m × ΔH_f

Where:

  • ΔH_f = Heat of fusion (latent heat) (J/kg)
  • Occurs at constant T_melt
EXERGY FROM LATENT HEAT

B = Q × (1 - T₀/T_melt)

Simpler than sensible heat because T is constant during phase change

PCM Requirements for TES

  • Melting point: Must match application temperature range
  • High ΔH_f: More energy per kg
  • High thermal conductivity: Faster charging/discharging
  • Small volume change: Avoid container damage
  • Thermal stability: No degradation over many cycles
  • Non-toxic, non-flammable: Safety
  • Low cost: Economic viability

💡 Sensible vs Latent Heat Storage

Advantages of Latent (PCM):

  • Higher energy density (more energy per kg/m³)
  • Constant temperature during phase change (good for some applications)

Disadvantages of Latent:

  • Poor thermal conductivity limits heat transfer rate
  • More expensive materials
  • Exergetic loss if HTF temperature >> T_melt

Worked Example (EP2016, Q7 - 60 marks total)

Question: 30 tonnes KNO₃ (potassium nitrate) cycled between 305-395°C. Properties: ΔH_f = 103 kJ/kg, c_p,solid = 1.43 kJ/kg·K, c_p,liquid = 1.37 kJ/kg·K, T_melt = 337°C (610K). Calculate total energy and exergy (T₀ = 298K).

Solution - Total Energy:

  • E_total = Q_solid + Q_latent + Q_liquid
  • Q_solid = 30,000 × 1.43 × (610-578) = 1.373 MJ
  • Q_latent = 30,000 × 103 = 3.09 MJ
  • Q_liquid = 30,000 × 1.37 × (668-610) = 2.384 MJ
  • Total = 6.85 GJ

Solution - Exergy (more complex):

  • B_solid = m×c_p×[ΔT - T₀×ln(T₂/T₁)] = 0.684 GJ
  • B_latent = Q×(1-T₀/T_melt) = 3.09×(1-298/610) = 1.58 GJ
  • B_liquid = m×c_p×[ΔT - T₀×ln(T₂/T₁)] = 0.910 GJ
  • Total Exergy = 3.24 GJ (vs 6.85 GJ energy!)

Key lesson: Only 47% of stored energy is exergy! The rest cannot do useful work.

Applications

  • Concentrated Solar Power (CSP): Molten salt storage at 250-650°C
  • A-CAES: Stores compression heat for later expansion
  • District heating: Large hot water tanks
  • Industrial waste heat recovery: PCM or sensible storage

🔵 Not Previously Tested: Thermochemical storage (reversible reactions like MgH₂ ↔ Mg + H₂), detailed thermocline calculations, specific CSP plant configurations

Electrochemical Energy Storage Devices (EESDs)

Convert chemical energy directly to electrical energy through redox reactions. Include batteries (enclosed system), flow batteries (external electrolyte tanks), supercapacitors (surface charge storage), and fuel cells (continuous fuel supply).

2.1 LEAD-ACID BATTERIES

Lead-Acid Batteries

⚠️ HIGHLY TESTED - ELECTROCHEMISTRY FUNDAMENTALS

Tested in: 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, 2020, 2021, 2022, 2023

Marks: 20-30 per question | Focus: Half-reactions, Gibbs free energy, theoretical capacity calculations

Lead-Acid Battery

Invented by Gaston Planté in 1859. First rechargeable (secondary) battery. Still widely used for vehicle SLI (Starting, Lighting, Ignition) and backup power despite low specific energy (30-50 Wh/kg) because of low cost and reliability.

⭐ Electrochemical Reactions (MUST MEMORIZE)

LEAD-ACID BATTERY REACTIONS (DISCHARGE →)

Negative electrode (anode):

Pb + HSO₄⁻ → PbSO₄ + H⁺ + 2e⁻

Positive electrode (cathode):

PbO₂ + 3H⁺ + HSO₄⁻ + 2e⁻ → PbSO₄ + 2H₂O

Overall reaction:

PbO₂ + Pb + 2H₂SO₄ ⇌ 2PbSO₄ + 2H₂O

Key points: n = 2 electrons, E°_cell = 2.0 V, MW_reactants = 643 g/mol

Key Characteristics

ParameterValue
Cell voltage2.0 V (6 cells in series = 12V car battery)
Specific energy30-50 Wh/kg
Cycle life300-1000 cycles
Self-discharge5-15% per month
Operating temp-20°C to +60°C
Cost$50-150/kWh (lowest of all batteries)

Advantages & Disadvantages

✅ Advantages:

  • Low cost and simple to manufacture
  • Wide operating temperature range
  • Good high-current performance
  • Established recycling infrastructure

❌ Disadvantages:

  • Low specific energy (heavy for energy stored)
  • Limited cycle life
  • Slow charging (12-16 hours typically)
  • Must be stored charged (sulfation if discharged)
  • Produces H₂ and O₂ gas (needs ventilation)
  • Lead is highly toxic (environmental concern)

Side Reactions (Causes of Degradation)

  • Water electrolysis: H₂O → ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ (at positive); 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (at negative)
  • Sulfation: Crystalline PbSO₄ forms if discharged too long (irreversible)
  • Self-discharge: Slow conversion of active materials without external circuit

🔵 Less Commonly Tested: Detailed degradation mechanisms, specific construction details, lead-acid flow batteries

2.2 LITHIUM-ION BATTERIES

Lithium-Ion Batteries

⚠️ EXTREMELY HIGH FREQUENCY - CALCULATIONS ESSENTIAL

Tested in: Every year 2015-2025 (11 consecutive years!)

Marks: 20-50 per question | Must know: Half-reactions, capacity calculations, Nernst equation

Lithium-Ion Battery

First commercialized by Sony in 1991. Revolutionized portable electronics and now dominates EV market. High specific energy (150-250 Wh/kg), long cycle life (1000-5000 cycles), but more expensive and requires sophisticated battery management system (BMS).

⭐⭐⭐ Electrochemical Reactions (MEMORIZE MULTIPLE CATHODE TYPES)

LiFePO₄ (Lithium Iron Phosphate) - MOST COMMON IN EXAMS

Negative electrode (anode) during discharge:

LiC₆ → 6C + Li⁺ + e⁻

Positive electrode (cathode) during discharge:

FePO₄ + Li⁺ + e⁻ → LiFePO₄

Overall:

LiC₆ + FePO₄ → 6C + LiFePO₄

Key: n=1, E°=3.2V, MW_reactants=230 g/mol

LiCoO₂ (Lithium Cobalt Oxide) - Also Tested

Overall: Li₁₋ₓCoO₂ + LiₓC₆ → LiCoO₂ + 6C

Key: n=1, E°=4.0V (higher voltage!), MW=170 g/mol

NMC811 (LiNi₀.₈Mn₀.₁Co₀.₁O₂) - Recent Exams

Overall: Ni₀.₈Mn₀.₁Co₀.₁O₂ + LiC₆ → LiNi₀.₈Mn₀.₁Co₀.₁O₂ + 6C

Key: n=1, E°=3.8V, MW=169 g/mol

⭐⭐⭐ CRITICAL FORMULAS FOR BATTERY CALCULATIONS

Gibbs Free Energy & Cell Potential

ΔG° = -nFE°_cell

Where:

  • n = number of electrons transferred per mole
  • F = Faraday constant = 96,485 C/mol
  • E°_cell = Standard cell potential (V)
⭐⭐⭐ THEORETICAL SPECIFIC CAPACITY

Specific Capacity = nF / MW

Units: mAh/g or Ah/kg

Conversion: 1 Ah = 3600 C

So: Capacity (Ah/mol) = nF/3600 = n × 96485/3600 = n × 26.8

⭐⭐⭐ THEORETICAL SPECIFIC ENERGY

Specific Energy = (nF × E_cell) / MW

Units: Wh/kg

Shortcut: Specific Energy = Specific Capacity × Voltage

⚠️ UNIT CONVERSION CRITICAL!

1 Ah = 3600 C

1 Wh = 3600 J

MW must be in grams/mol for mAh/g or kg/mol for Ah/kg

Worked Example (EP2015 - 60 marks, appears similarly in many exams)

Question: For LiFePO₄ battery: E°=3.2V, n=1, MW_reactants=230 g/mol. Calculate theoretical specific capacity and specific energy.

Solution:

  • Step 1 - Capacity:
    • nF = 1 × 96,485 = 96,485 C/mol
    • In Ah: 96,485/3600 = 26.8 Ah/mol
    • Specific capacity = 26.8/0.230 = 117 Ah/kg = 117 mAh/g
  • Step 2 - Energy:
    • Method 1: Specific energy = Capacity × Voltage = 117 × 3.2 = 374 Wh/kg
    • Method 2: nFE/MW = (96485 × 3.2)/0.230 = 1.344×10⁶ J/kg = 373 Wh/kg ✓

Nernst Equation (For Non-Standard Conditions)

Nernst Equation

E_cell = E°_cell - (RT/nF) × ln(Q)

Where:

  • R = 8.314 J/(mol·K)
  • T = Temperature (K)
  • Q = Reaction quotient (products/reactants)

At 25°C: E = E° - (0.0257/n) × ln(Q)

Why is practical energy lower than theoretical?

  • Inactive materials (current collectors, separator, casing, BMS) add weight
  • Not all active material participates in reaction
  • Lower actual voltage due to internal resistance
  • Safety margins prevent full depth of discharge

C-Rate Concept

C-rate defines charging/discharging speed:

  • 1C = full discharge in 1 hour (current = capacity in Ah)
  • 0.5C = full discharge in 2 hours
  • 2C = full discharge in 30 minutes

Example: 4750 mAh battery at 1C draws 4.75A. At 0.2C draws 0.95A. At 2C draws 9.5A.

Higher C-rates reduce available capacity due to polarization losses!

⭐ Effects of C-Rate on Battery Performance (EP2025)

⚠️ NEW IN 2025 EXAM - LIKELY TO REPEAT

Question type: Plot discharge curves at different C-rates, explain capacity reduction

As C-rate increases (faster discharge):

EffectReason
Available capacity DECREASES Incomplete utilization of active material, concentration gradients, kinetic limitations
Terminal voltage DROPS Higher current → larger I×Rinternal voltage drop
Power output INCREASES P = V×I, higher current despite lower voltage
Energy losses INCREASE Heat dissipation Ploss = I²R increases with I²
Temperature RISES More heat generation from internal resistance

Typical Capacity Reduction with C-Rate

  • 0.2C: ~100% of rated capacity available
  • 1C: ~90-95% of rated capacity
  • 2C: ~80-85% of rated capacity
  • >5C: Can drop to 60-70% of rated capacity

Discharge Curve Characteristics

Voltage vs State of Charge at different C-rates:

  • Lower C-rates (0.2C): Relatively flat discharge plateau, voltage stays high longer
  • Higher C-rates (2C): Steeper voltage drop, earlier cutoff voltage reached
  • All curves eventually converge at low SOC as kinetics become uniformly slow

💡 Exam Tip for C-rate Questions:

When asked to sketch discharge curves:

  • X-axis: Time (or Capacity in Ah)
  • Y-axis: Voltage (V)
  • Draw 3 curves (e.g., 0.2C, 1C, 2C)
  • Higher C-rate curves:
    • Reach cutoff voltage faster (shorter time)
    • Have lower average voltage throughout
    • Deliver less total capacity (Ah)

State of Charge (SOC) & Depth of Discharge (DOD)

  • SOC: Percentage of full charge remaining (100% = fully charged)
  • DOD: Percentage of capacity discharged (DOD = 100% - SOC)
  • Shallow cycling: Limited DOD (e.g., 0-30%) extends battery life
  • Deep cycling: High DOD (e.g., 0-80%+) reduces cycle life but uses more capacity

🔵 Not Extensively Tested: Detailed SEI layer formation, dendrite growth mechanisms, specific BMS algorithms, thermal runaway detailed chemistry

2.3 ELECTRIC DOUBLE-LAYER CAPACITORS (EDLCs)

Supercapacitors

⚠️ HIGHLY TESTED - SPECIFIC FORMULAS

Tested in: 2018, 2020, 2021A, 2022, 2023, 2024

Marks: 14-30 per question | Focus: Capacitance calculation from CV curves, specific energy/power

Electric Double-Layer Capacitor (EDLC)

Stores energy electrostatically through ion adsorption at electrode-electrolyte interface (NOT chemical reactions like batteries). Provides very high power (kW/kg) but low energy (1-10 Wh/kg). Can charge/discharge in seconds for 500,000-1,000,000 cycles!

⭐⭐⭐ CRITICAL SUPERCAPACITOR FORMULAS

Capacitance from CV Curve

C = i / ν

Where:

  • C = Capacitance (F)
  • i = Current from CV curve (A)
  • ν = Potential scan rate (V/s)

Example: If CV shows i=2A at scan rate 1mV/s: C = 2/(0.001) = 2000F

⭐ ENERGY STORED

E = ½ C U²

Where U = maximum voltage (V)

Specific Energy: E_specific = E/mass (Wh/kg)

⭐⭐ MAXIMUM SPECIFIC POWER

P_max = U² / (4 × ESR × mass)

Where:

  • ESR = Equivalent Series Resistance (Ω)
  • Lower ESR → Higher power capability

Worked Example (EP2021A, Q4 - 30 marks)

Question: CV curve shows i=2.0A at ν=1mV/s, U=3.0V, ESR=0.60mΩ, mass=220g. Find specific energy and specific power.

Solution:

  • Step 1 - Capacitance: C = i/ν = 2.0/0.001 = 2000 F
  • Step 2 - Energy: E = ½CU² = ½×2000×3² = 9000 J = 2.5 Wh
  • Step 3 - Specific Energy: 2.5/0.22 = 11.4 Wh/kg
  • Step 4 - Specific Power: P = 3²/(4×0.0006×0.22) = 17.0 kW/kg

Key insight: 1500× higher specific power than energy! Perfect for power applications.

2.4 REDOX FLOW BATTERIES & FUEL CELLS

Flow Batteries & Fuel Cells

Redox Flow Batteries (RFBs)

⚠️ MODERATELY TESTED - UNDERSTANDING PRINCIPLES

Tested in: 2016, 2018, 2020, 2021

Marks: 10-20 per question | Focus: Difference from conventional batteries, advantages/disadvantages

Redox Flow Battery

Electroactive species dissolved in external electrolyte tanks. Energy capacity determined by tank size (scalable!), power by electrode stack size. Can independently tune energy and power.

⭐ Vanadium Redox Flow Battery (VRFB) - Most Common

VRFB Electrochemistry

Positive electrode: VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + e⁻ ⇌ VO²⁺ + H₂O

Negative electrode: V²⁺ ⇌ V³⁺ + e⁻

Overall: VO₂⁺ + 2H⁺ + V²⁺ ⇌ VO²⁺ + V³⁺ + H₂O

Cell voltage: 1.26 V

Key Advantages of Flow Batteries

  • Independent power/energy scaling: Tank size ≠ stack size
  • Long cycle life: No electrode degradation (electrolyte replacement possible)
  • Deep discharge OK: No damage from 100% DOD
  • No self-discharge: When pumps off
  • Room temperature operation: No thermal management complexity

Disadvantages

  • Low energy density: Large footprint for given energy
  • Complex system: Pumps, tanks, plumbing
  • Cross-contamination: Gradual mixing through membrane
  • Cost: Expensive for small-scale, competitive for MW-scale grid storage

Fuel Cells

Fuel Cell

Electrochemical device that continuously converts chemical energy of fuel (usually H₂) directly to electricity. NOT energy storage (unless H₂ is stored), but energy conversion. High efficiency (40-60%, or >85% with CHP).

⭐ Proton Exchange Membrane Fuel Cell (PEMFC)

PEMFC Reactions

Anode: H₂ → 2H⁺ + 2e⁻

Cathode: ½O₂ + 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂O

Overall: H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O

Operating temp: ~80°C, requires high purity H₂ (99.999%)

Fuel Cell Efficiency vs Temperature

Thermodynamic Efficiency

η = ΔG / ΔH

At 25°C: ΔH = -285.83 kJ/mol, ΔG = -237.13 kJ/mol

η = 237.13/285.83 = 83% (theoretical maximum)

Actual fuel cells: 40-60% electrical, >85% with combined heat & power

3.1 HYDROGEN STORAGE

Hydrogen Storage

⚠️ FREQUENTLY TESTED - PRESSURE VESSELS & UNDERGROUND STORAGE

Tested in: 2015, 2017, 2018, 2021A, 2023, 2024

Marks: 20-40 per question | Focus: Tank calculations, underground storage types, pressure exergy

Hydrogen Storage Challenges

H₂ has highest specific energy (142 MJ/kg) but lowest volumetric energy density due to low molecular weight. Storage options: compressed gas (700 bar vehicles), liquid (-253°C), metal hydrides, or underground for large-scale.

High-Pressure Compressed H₂ Storage

⭐ Ideal Gas Density

ρ = p / (RT)

Where:

  • R = Specific gas constant for H₂ = 4124 J/(kg·K)
  • p = Pressure (Pa)
  • T = Temperature (K)

Example: At 700 bar, 300K: ρ = (700×10⁵)/(4124×300) = 56.6 kg/m³

⭐ Pressure Vessel Wall Thickness (Thin-Walled)

t = (p × r) / σ_max

Where:

  • t = Wall thickness (m)
  • p = Internal pressure (Pa)
  • r = Internal radius (m)
  • σ_max = Maximum allowable stress (Pa)
Throttling Exergy Loss

B_loss = p₀V₀ × [ln(p₁/p₀) - ln(p₂/p₀)]

When H₂ is throttled from high pressure p₁ to lower pressure p₂, exergy is destroyed (irreversible process with no work extraction)

Worked Example (EP2021A, Q2 - 30 marks)

Question: Vehicle needs 60 kWh output from H₂ engine (η=50%). H₂ stored at 700 bar, 300K. Calculate mass needed, tank volume, throttling exergy loss (to 7 bar), tank weight (r=125mm, σ_max=700MPa, ρ_steel=5000 kg/m³).

Solution:

  • Step 1 - H₂ mass: Need 120 kWh heat = 432 MJ. Mass = 432/142 = 3.04 kg
  • Step 2 - Density: ρ = (700×10⁵)/(4124×300) = 56.58 kg/m³
  • Step 3 - Volume (99% usable): V = 3.04/(0.99×56.58) = 0.0543 m³ = 54.3 L
  • Step 4 - Throttling loss: B = 101325×(3.04/56.58)×[ln(700)-ln(7)] = 21.1 MJ = 5.85 kWh (10% of output!)
  • Step 5 - Wall thickness: t = (70×10⁶×0.125)/(700×10⁶) = 0.0125 m = 12.5 mm
  • Step 6 - Tank mass: V_steel = 2πrt×L ≈ 0.0114 m³, mass = 57 kg (19× the H₂!)

    • Created by solution mining
    • Gas-tight, high mechanical strength
    • Fast injection/withdrawal rates
    • Low cushion gas requirement (~30%)
    • Example: Hypster project (France), Krummhörn (Germany 700 MWh)
  • Depleted Oil/Gas Fields
    • Existing infrastructure
    • Largest capacity potential
    • Slower response than salt caverns
    • May contain hydrocarbon residues (contamination risk)
  • Aquifers
    • Most expensive to develop
    • Highest base gas requirement
    • Extensive testing needed
    • Advantage: No hydrocarbon contamination
  • Lined Rock Caverns (LRC)
    • Excavated hard rock + liner
    • Example: HYBRIT Sweden (100 m³, 250 bar)
    • Expensive but flexible location

3.2 GREEN AMMONIA STORAGE

Green Ammonia as Energy Carrier

⚠️ MODERATELY TESTED - CONCEPTUAL UNDERSTANDING

Tested in: 2021A, 2023, 2024

Marks: 20-30 per question | Focus: Haber-Bosch process, advantages vs H₂, electrochemical production

Green Ammonia

NH₃ produced using renewable electricity (via electrolytic H₂ + Haber-Bosch). Acts as hydrogen carrier with 40% energy density of petrol. Easier to store/transport than pure H₂ (liquid at -33°C or 10-15 bar vs -253°C or 700 bar for H₂).

⭐ Haber-Bosch Process (Industrial Production)

Haber-Bosch Chemistry

N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃ + Heat (ΔH = -92 kJ/mol)

Conditions:

  • Temperature: 300-550°C (kinetic compromise)
  • Pressure: 100-300 bar (equilibrium favors high P)
  • Catalyst: Iron-based (or Ruthenium for better performance)

Why high T despite exothermic? N₂ triple bond is very strong, requires high activation energy. Reaction is thermodynamically favored at low T but kinetically too slow without high T.

Electrochemical Ammonia Production (Emerging)

Electrochemical Route

Anode: 3H₂ → 6H⁺ + 6e⁻

Cathode: N₂ + 6H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2NH₃

Overall: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

Potential advantages: Lower temperature, modular, renewable electricity direct integration. Still developmental!

4.1 COAL STORAGE & SPONTANEOUS COMBUSTION

Coal Storage

⚠️ TESTED IN EARLY YEARS (2014-2017)

Tested in: 2014, 2015, 2016, 2017

Marks: 15-20 per question | Types: List factors, explain prevention measures, explain chimney effect

Spontaneous Combustion of Coal

A major hazard in long-term coal storage where coal self-heats through slow oxidation, potentially leading to fires. Understanding intrinsic (coal properties) and extrinsic (environmental) factors is critical for safe storage operations.

Intrinsic Factors (Coal Properties)

FactorEffect on Spontaneous Combustion
Coal Rank MOST IMPORTANT. Lower rank coals (sub-bituminous, lignite) are MORE susceptible than higher rank coals (bituminous, anthracite)
Moisture Content Two-fold effect: (1) High moisture acts as heat sink (reduces risk), (2) Moisture adsorption releases "heat of wetting" (increases risk). Low moisture coals more prone to spontaneous combustion
Volatile Matter Higher volatile content increases susceptibility to self-heating
Mineral Matter (esp. Pyrite) Pyrite (FeS₂) oxidation is STRONGLY EXOTHERMIC even at ambient conditions, dramatically accelerates coal heating. Critical concern!
Particle Size Smaller particles = larger surface area = more oxidation sites = higher risk. Also increases void capacity for air flow

Extrinsic Factors (Environmental/Climatic)

FactorImpact
Air Humidity & Pressure Fluctuations trigger moisture adsorption/re-adsorption, releasing significant "heat of wetting" (can increase pile temperature by 30°C!)
Rainfall Beyond humidity effects, heavy rain creates void channels (water pathways) allowing air ingress into oxygen-depleted stockpiles
Ambient Temperature High ambient T enhances oxidation rate. Large temperature variations create natural convection ("chimney effect")
Wind MAJOR climatic factor. Enhances air convection, drives fresh oxygen deeper into pile, causes drying/weathering, fluctuations in moisture content

⭐ The "Chimney Effect"

Chimney Effect

Movement of air into and out of coal stockpiles due to natural convection caused by temperature differences between ambient air and the stockpile interior. This significantly affects airflow pattern and velocity, promoting coal oxidation and temperature rises.

Prevention Measures for Chimney Effect:

  • Avoid particle size segregation during stacking (maintains uniform density)
  • Compact the "toe" of stockpile to prevent air flow in/out of bottom when interior is warmer/cooler than ambient

Prevention & Best Practices

  • Site Preparation: Level, well-drained base, away from ignition sources
  • Stockpile Formation: Proper stacking methodology, avoid segregation
  • Regular Compaction: Reduces void spaces, limits air penetration
  • Wind Shielding: Physical barriers to reduce wind-driven air convection
  • Surface Coverage: Tarpaulins, spray-on coatings, or vegetation to limit oxygen access
  • Wet Suppression: Water spray/cannon (with/without wetting agents) to increase surface moisture
  • Temperature Monitoring: Regular thermographic surveys to detect hot spots early
  • Don't mix different coals: Especially those with significantly different moisture contents or pyrite levels
📝 Exam Strategy for Coal Questions

Typical question patterns:

  • List intrinsic/extrinsic factors (15 marks): Give factor name + brief explanation of effect
  • Prevention measures (5-6 marks): List 5-6 measures with short explanation
  • Chimney effect (4 marks): Define (2 marks) + prevention measures (2 marks)
  • Can two coals be stored together? (10 marks): Compare properties from table, focus on moisture, pyrite, volatile matter differences

4.2 INTERNAL RESISTANCE OF BATTERIES

Internal Resistance

⚠️ IMPORTANT CONCEPTUAL TOPIC

Tested in: 2014, 2016

Marks: 6-20 per question | Focus: Maximum power transfer, energy efficiency

Internal Resistance (RI)

All electrochemical energy storage devices (EESDs) - batteries, supercapacitors - have internal resistance representing energy losses from ionic transport through electrolyte, charge transfer resistance at electrodes, and electronic resistance of current collectors.

Effects of Internal Resistance

  • Voltage drop during discharge: Vterminal = Vopen-circuit - I×RI
  • Heat generation: Ploss = I²RI
  • Reduced available energy: Energy wasted as heat
  • Power limitation: Limits maximum current that can be drawn
⭐ Maximum Power Transfer Condition
RL = RI

Where:

  • RL = Load resistance
  • RI = Internal resistance

When RL = RI: Maximum power delivered to load, but only 50% efficiency!

High Efficiency Condition (95%+ Energy Delivery)
RL >> RI

For 95% energy efficiency: RL ≈ 19×RI

Trade-off: Higher efficiency but lower power output

Worked Example (EP2014, Q3)

Question: For maximum power transfer, what relationship between RL and RI? For 95% energy delivery, how should this change?

Solution:

  • Maximum power: RL = RI (matched impedance)
  • Power delivered: PL = V²/(4RI), Efficiency = 50%
  • For 95% energy delivery: Energy losses = I²RIt, must be < 5% of total
  • This requires RL >> RI, specifically RL ≈ 19RI
4.3 CAPACITORS, INDUCTORS & SMES

Alternative Energy Storage Technologies

⚠️ TESTED 2015-2016

Marks: 4-12 per question | Focus: Energy density limits, fundamental principles

Simple Foil Capacitors

Energy Stored in Capacitor
E = ½ C V2 = 12 ε Efield2 × Volume

What limits energy per unit volume in foil capacitor?

  • Breakdown field strength (Emax): Maximum electric field before dielectric fails (~100 MV/m typical)
  • Permittivity (ε): ε = ε₀ × εr, higher εr stores more energy

Energy density example: εr=5, Emax=100 MV/m

Energy density ≈ ½ × (ε₀εr) × Emax² ≈ 800 kJ/m³ ≈ 300 J/kg

Compare to kerosene: 46 MJ/kg! Capacitors have VERY low energy density.

Simple Inductors (Toroidal)

Energy in Inductor
E = ½ L I2 = B22μ0 × Volume

What limits energy per unit volume in inductor?

  • Current density limit: Typically ~2 A/mm² for copper wire (overheating concern)
  • Magnetic flux density (B): Limited by how much current can pass through torus center
  • B = μ₀Itotal/(2πRmean) for toroid

For iron-core inductors: Most energy stored in small "airgap" (thickness d). Energy confined to tiny volume while overall inductor is large → very poor energy density.

Typical specific energy for copper inductor: ~0.03 J/kg

Extremely poor! Inductors are NOT competitive for bulk energy storage.

Superconducting Magnetic Energy Storage (SMES)

SMES

Uses superconducting coils (zero electrical resistance below critical temperature Tc) to store energy in magnetic field. No resistive losses during storage, but requires cryogenic cooling (liquid N₂ at 77K or liquid He at 4K).

Two limits governing SMES energy storage:

  1. Current density in superconductor: Maximum current that can flow without exceeding critical current density
  2. Magnetic field perpendicular to current: Superconductivity breaks down above critical magnetic field strength perpendicular to current direction

Advantages:

  • Negligible standby losses (superconductor has ~zero resistance)
  • Very fast response time (milliseconds)
  • High power density
  • Long lifetime (>20 years)

Disadvantages:

  • Extremely high cost per kWh (highest of all storage technologies)
  • Requires continuous cryogenic cooling
  • Complex system
  • Specialist applications only (grid stabilization, pulse power)

4.4 UNDERGROUND GAS STORAGE (DETAILED CALCULATIONS)

Underground Gas Storage Engineering

⚠️ DETAILED CALCULATIONS - SECTION B QUESTIONS

Tested in: 2014, 2020, 2022

Marks: 60-100 marks (full Section B question) | Very calculation-heavy!

Key Concepts

Working Gas vs Cushion Gas:

  • Working Gas: Gas that can be withdrawn and used
  • Cushion Gas (Base Gas): Gas that must remain to maintain pressure/deliverability
  • Total Gas in Place = Working Gas + Cushion Gas
⭐ Formation Volume Factor (CRITICAL)
Bg = 0.0283 × ZTp

Where:

  • Bg = Formation volume factor (m³/sm³)
  • Z = Gas compressibility factor (dimensionless, ~0.8-1.0)
  • T = Absolute temperature (K)
  • p = Pressure (bar or MPa - watch units!)

Relates: Volume at standard conditions (sm³) to volume at reservoir conditions (m³)

Gas in Place Calculation
Gs = VreservoirBg

Where Gs = gas volume at standard conditions (sm³ or Msm³)

Pressure-Volume Relationships

Material Balance Equation
pZ = m × Gs + c

Linear relationship! Slope m and intercept c determined from operational data.

From (p/Z) vs Gs graph:

  • Slope gives rate of pressure change with gas injection/withdrawal
  • Intercept at Gs=0 gives initial (p/Z)
  • Can extrapolate to find maximum capacity at maximum pressure

Injectivity & Deliverability

Darcy Flow Equation (Radial, Gas)
qinj = kh(pinj2 - p2)1424μZT[ln(0.472re/rw) + S]

Where:

  • qinj = Injection rate (Mscf/day or Kscf/day)
  • k = Permeability (mD or Darcy)
  • h = Formation thickness (ft)
  • pinj = Wellbore injection pressure (psia)
  • p̄ = Average reservoir pressure (psia)
  • μ̄, Z̄ = Average viscosity, compressibility
  • re = Drainage radius (ft)
  • rw = Wellbore radius (ft)
  • S = Skin factor (damage/stimulation, dimensionless)

Skin Factor (S):

  • S = 0: No damage/stimulation
  • S > 0: Formation damage (reduces injectivity)
  • S < 0: Formation stimulation (increases injectivity)

⚠️ UNIT HELL!

Underground gas storage calculations use oilfield units (psia, ft, mD, °F). Be EXTREMELY careful with unit conversions!

  • 1 bar = 14.504 psia
  • 1 MPa = 145.04 psia
  • T(°F) = 1.8×T(°C) + 32, T(°R) = T(°F) + 460
  • 1 ft = 0.3048 m
📝 Underground Storage Exam Strategy

These are LONG calculation questions (60+ marks). Approach systematically:

  1. Read data table carefully - extract p/Z values and cumulative volumes
  2. Plot p/Z vs Gs if asked - linear fit determines slope/intercept
  3. Apply material balance to find initial gas in place
  4. Calculate Bg at different conditions for capacity calculations
  5. Use Darcy equation for injectivity/deliverability (watch units!)
  6. Show ALL working - partial marks available

Thermo-Mechanical Storage

Pumped Hydro

E = mgh

P = ρgQhη

Flywheels

Adiabatic: T₂/T₁ = (p₂/p₁)^((γ-1)/γ), γ=1.4 for air

Thermal Storage

Sensible: Q = mc_pΔT

Latent: Q = mΔH_f

Exergy (sensible): B = mc_p[ΔT - T₀×ln(T₂/T₁)]

Exergy (latent): B = Q(1-T₀/T_melt)

Electrochemical Storage

Batteries

ΔG° = -nFE°_cell

Specific Capacity = nF/MW (Ah/kg)

Specific Energy = nFE_cell/MW (Wh/kg)

Nernst: E = E° - (RT/nF)×ln(Q)

Key values: F=96,485 C/mol, 1 Ah=3600 C

Supercapacitors

C = i/ν

E = ½CU²

P_max = U²/(4×ESR×mass)

Hydrogen & Gas Storage

Ideal Gas & Storage

Universal Concepts

Energy vs Exergy

Carnot Efficiency: η = 1 - T₀/T_hot

Exergy from Heat: B = Q(1-T₀/T_heat)

Remember: Electricity = 100% exergy, Heat exergy depends on T!
